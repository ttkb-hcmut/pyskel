TITLE: Semantics in Skel and Necro
AUTHORS: Louis Noizet and Alan Schmitt

----------- Overall evaluation -----------
SCORE: 0 (borderline paper)
----- TEXT:
SUMMARY

This paper describes a domain specific meta-language (Skel) for encoding
language semantics and associated tool suite (Neco): translators to OCaml (for
interpreters) and to Coq (for reasoning).

The Skel language itself can be viewed as a restricted version of a
polymorphic functional programming language, augmented with a
non-deterministic pattern matching. The restrictions ensure that all semantic
functions defined in Skel can be translated to both OCaml and Coq in a
specified way.  The nondeterminism in Skel provides a built-in semantics for
implementing nondeterministic choice in the dynamic semantics.

The translation to OCaml is via a mixed embedding, where the "skeleton" of
the expression is embedded shallowly but the terms are embedded deeply.

The translation to Coq maps Skel's partial functions to Coq's inductive
relations.

Overall, this paper sits in a crowded design space and the paper could do a
better job at establishing its contributions and explaining the rationale for
its design decisions. The related work section does not provide many details
other than noting that Skel/Neco is simpler. While simplicity is a virtue, it
would be good to know where this simplicity comes from: is it that the tool is
less mature? or that it targets a narrower application domain?  Or is there
some aspect of the design that leads to simplicity. If so, what?

DETAILED COMMENTS

- line 22. Please do not make unsupported claims like "it is easy to
understand its semantics". Broad claims about human cognition require user
studies to back them up.

- line 46. Please elaborate on the contributions of your artifacts. What is novel
about their design? What can we learn from your experience?

- Figure 1. Are type declarations required to be strictly positive? Could I define
a Skel datatype with embedded functions?

- Figure 1. Why are only the decl keywords red in the figure? What does the
blue/green coloring mean in the examples?

- line 159. Isn't nontermination another source of partiality?

- What is the motivation for the inclusion of nondeterminism (and implicitly
nontermination) in your language for describing semantics? Why are these two
effects important for the specification of interpreters? What about others?

- Your description of the mixed embedding in 4.2 (shallow embedding for
types/terms, deep embedding for skeletons) sounds a lot like free monad based
approaches, which have seen a lot of attention recently. Is there a
connection? But then section 4.2.2 sounds like you are using a shallow
embedding for the monads instead. Can you clarify what you mean?

- Have you considered using techniques such as free/freer monads (or variants
like interaction trees) in your translation to Coq? An advantage of this
approach is that it could make your Coq semantics more similar to your OCaml
definitions.

- Similar work sometimes includes a tool for preparing write-ups and
explaining language semantics using standard mathematical notation. In
other words, tools that translate the DSL to LaTeX or some other typesetting
system. Please don't undervalue this step of defining language semantics---it
is important to be able to communicate with users who do not know the Skel
language.

- This tool focuses only on dynamic semantics and ignores the static semantics
(i.e. type system) of the object languages. Are there plans to also extend the
tool in this respect?

- Why is monad inlining important? Why not include the monad in the output?

Related work (Other DSLs for language semantics)
------------------------------------------------

Not only does the related work section provide a shallow comparison with tools
such as Lem and K, but it completely ignores many other systems. Not all of
the systems below provide both implementation (OCaml) and metatheoretic
definitions (Coq), but they do include DSLs for one or the other. It would be
good to compare these DSLs against Skel's design more thoroughly. (And if
these systems are not related, it would be good to say why in order to better
define the design space that your tool addresses.)

SCORE: 2 (accept)
----- TEXT:
The paper introduces the Skel+Necro environment for the specification, execution, and proofs about semantics of programming languages. The Skel language is based on one of the author's previous work [1], which included a Coq
formalization. The present paper builds upon that work, by extending the language, defining an ML-like syntax, and implementing an impressive framework around it which enables one to extract an OCaml interpreter (pending the definition
of unspecified objects) and Coq specification. The Coq specification is supported by three different semantics for Skel, which gives users the flexibility to chose. All three semantics are proved to be equivalent in Coq, ensuring that
one can confidently change between them.

The paper is generally well written, with many examples and code snippets at the right places to help understanding. The implementation involves a non-trivial amount of software engineering, and the authors did a good job organizing the
files in the repository. Each module includes detailed instructions and most can be installed via opam.

[1] Martin Bodin, Philippa Gardner, Thomas Jensen, Alan Schmitt. Skeletal Semantics and their Interpretations. Proceedings of the ACM on Programming Languages, ACM, In press, 44, pp.1-31. (https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/3290357)

----------- Questions for authors -----------
I would appreciate if the authors describe in more detail the additions to the language w.r.t. the previous work "Skeletal Semantics and their Interpretations". Were polymorphism and monads the only features added? How does this affect
the properties proved in Coq about skeleton semantics? Did you have to adapt or extend any proofs?

Would you agree to change the category of your paper from a regular research paper to a system description, if the program committee feels that this change is warranted?

----- TEXT:
The paper presents the meta language Skel and how it can be used to
describe the semantics of programming languages. The tools associated
to the language can be used to manipulate the semantics specifications
and, in particular, to generate OCaml interpreters and Coq
formalizations for the specified languages.

Presenting the formalism and the corresponding tools in the same paper
is quite ambitious given the space restrictions since it limits the
discussions for the two parts. This is particularly problematic here
for the formalism and presenting the rules in the appendix is not a
reasonable option, in my opinion. The claim that the formalism can be
used to "faithfully [...] capture informal specifications" should be
supported by the formalisation of (at least some of) the rules, which
should thus be present in the main paper.  As far as it concerns the
tools, Section 4 gives a nice overview although I'd have expected more
comments and/or examples on the (meta-theory) properties that are (or
intended to be) proved.

I give in what follows some more detailed comments.

The introduction is more or less a brief overview of the language and
of the tools presented in the paper. Even if the general motivations
for such a language and especially for the proof possibilities offered
by the Coq backend are quite obvious, it would have been nice to have
a more extended discussion on the specific goals of this relatively
new approach (comparing to existing ones), some examples of properties
targeted in general or for some specific cases, and possibly a flavor
of the language.

The presentation of Skel through an example is nice although some
points should be clarified here or later on for the sake of
consistency. For example, in eval-stmt, we don't know what's the
purpose of state and the syntax doesn't really correspond to the
syntax in Figure 1 (if we suppose x stands for a variable and not a
more complex statement). Bool comes also out of nowhere. It is not
clear what's the intent of the remark on lines 78-79 which looks
somewhat contradictory with the fact that branch deals with
non-determinism; probably giving the whole term would be more useful.

In the description of the formalism it would be better to explicitly
specify the set of variables, of constructors, of field names (which
are not even mentioned), of base types, and say they are
disjoint. Moreover, the x in Term Decl doesn't look like a variable
and the syntax is not consistent with the example neg (after line
73). The notation conventions should be used consistently with indexes
(like lines 123,..., and Appendix) or without (like in Figure 1);
personally I prefer the former.  The remark concerning the separation
of values and computations (starting on line 91) is not very clear - it
suggests terms in Figure 1 are values but according to Appendix B this
doesn't seem to be the case.  I think using always the ret would be
better - it would avoid the remark on line 98, and omitting it looks
sometimes somewhat strange (like at the bottom of page 7 where it's
not specified in the result of the transformation).  On line 97, I'd
say let-in binding to be consistent with the vocabulary in Section
2.4.

In Section 2.3, even if the typing rules are straightforward they
should be in the paper and at least briefly discussed. Defining ftype
and the partial function without giving the rules it's a bit awkward
and it's another argument for showing the rules in the main paper.

The same stands for the rules giving the concrete interpretation
(which are also mentioned in Section 4.3.4). In this case, commenting
on rule APP without showing it it's somewhat useless. The branch
disappears in Appendix B, probably because we have abstract syntax.
You could also mention explicitly here, or before, that the patterns
and the pattern matching are linear.

Concerning polymorphism, it should be make more clear that a,b at the
bottom of page 5 can be type variables, unlike the b in Figure 1.
Using the same identifier (eg b in map) for a term and a type might be
disturbing even if this is common in some communities.

On line 340, the eval_lv_ind property is mentioned but without any
discussion on its purpose the remark is quite useless.
